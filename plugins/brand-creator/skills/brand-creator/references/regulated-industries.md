## When

The brief names a profession whose promotion, naming or claims are regulated: law, medicine, dentistry, pharmacy,
veterinary care, psychology, finance, insurance, accounting and audit, tax advice, architecture, notaries and others.
Intake hands off here before round 1 ([00-intake](phases/00-intake.md), technique 7). The output is a dated rules
table in BRIEF.md and the Regulatory line of the strategy core's Guardrails block
([strategy-core.md](strategy-core.md)).

## The method (7 steps)

1. **Is the profession regulated in the target country?** Look it up in the EU Regulated Professions Database (for EU
   and EEA countries) or the national register of professions, and note the competent authority (the chamber, bar,
   council or regulator). Outside the EU, find the national licensing body.
2. **Find the professional body's own code.** The code of ethics or conduct is usually the binding rule for promotion,
   enforced through disciplinary proceedings. Look for its current consolidated text and for any resolution or
   guidance on "informing" versus "advertising". Read it at the body's own site (primary).
3. **Find the statute.** The act governing the profession, and the act for the entity when there is one (a medical
   entity, a law firm form, a regulated financial firm). Use the official legal-text service of the country.
4. **Cross-check general law.** Unfair-competition and consumer-protection rules apply on top, and in the EU the
   Services Directive (2006/123/EC, art. 24) requires member states to remove total bans on commercial communication
   by regulated professions while allowing proportionate rules on independence, dignity, integrity and professional
   secrecy. Read the article at the official EU source before relying on it.
5. **Check currency.** These codes change often. Record the code version or resolution number, the article, the
   exact article text (quoted in BRIEF.md, in the original language) and the date you read it. Anything taken from a
   commentary, a news article or a law-firm blog is secondary; anything you could not open at the primary source is
   UNVERIFIED.
6. **Turn rules into guardrails.** Each rule becomes one line the boards are tested against: copy register, claims,
   comparisons, testimonials, required name forms, mandatory disclosures, labelling of promotional content. Test every
   sample line on every board against them.
7. **Escalate interpretation.** Where a rule is open to reading (is this line "informing" or "advertising"?), state
   the question and both readings, and leave it with the owner or their adviser. You record; you do not give legal
   advice.

## Typical guardrails

Generic effects seen across professions (each must be confirmed for the actual profession and country):

- informative rather than promotional copy; promotional communication labelled as such;
- no comparative or evaluative claims ("best", "leading", "number one"), no claims about competitors;
- no client testimonials, or only with consent and never in certain matter types;
- no pressure, no guarantees of outcome, no fee-based referrals;
- a required formal name or designation used consistently (on the mark, the letterhead, the site and the book);
- mandatory disclosures (registration number, the supervising body, professional insurance) on given surfaces;
- limits on using the professional's image or authority to promote unrelated products.

These feed the Guardrails block, the "Forbidden" list in BRIEF.md (as exact phrases where they are words), and every
copy prompt.

## What stays with the owner

The owner (or their adviser) confirms the profession, the country, and every interpretation. The plugin records the
rules with dates and tests the boards against them; it does not decide what the law allows.
